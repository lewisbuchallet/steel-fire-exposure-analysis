# Compartment Fire Exposure and the Thermal Response of Unprotected Structural Steel

A comparison of three ways of describing a compartment fire — **ISO 834**, the **EN 1991-1-2 Annex A parametric fire** and a **Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS)** CFD model — and what each predicts for the temperature of an unprotected steel member, calculated with the **EN 1993-1-2** lumped-capacitance method. Each fire is also expressed as an equivalent time of ISO 834 exposure.

The full write-up is in [`Compartment_Fire_Exposure_and_Structural_Steel_Thermal_Response.pdf`](Compartment_Fire_Exposure_and_Structural_Steel_Thermal_Response.pdf).

## Summary of findings

For one idealised compartment (4 m × 4 m × 2.4 m, one 1.0 m × 2.0 m door, 0.2 m concrete boundaries) and an assumed section factor A_m/V = 200 m⁻¹:

| Exposure | Peak gas (°C) | Peak steel (°C) | Equivalent ISO 834 time (min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| EN 1991-1-2 Annex A parametric fire | 751 | 734 | 20.0 |
| FDS Run 1 (0.96 MW), above fire* | 826 | 728 | 18.9 |
| FDS Run 1 (0.96 MW), far ceiling* | 498 | 467 | 8.0 |
| FDS Run 2 (5 MW, matched to Annex A), above fire | 950 | 750 | 22.6 |
| FDS Run 2 (5 MW, matched to Annex A), far ceiling | 922 | 698 | 16.1 |

\*Run 1 never decays, so its steel temperature reaches a plateau rather than a peak.

- **Natural fires are far less severe than ISO 834 for longer ratings.** Under ISO 834 the steel is still heating after 3 hours (1109 °C). Under the natural fires it peaks and then cools. Across the three section factors studied (100, 200 and 300 m⁻¹), every natural-fire exposure gave an equivalent time of about 6 to 24 minutes.
- **The matched FDS fire and Annex A agree closely on steel temperature** (+16 K above the fire and −36 K at the far ceiling, at A_m/V = 200 m⁻¹), even though the FDS peak gas temperatures are 170–200 K higher. Steel temperature depends on the heat transferred over the whole exposure, not on the peak gas temperature alone.
- **FDS shows spatial variation that Annex A cannot represent:** about 50 K difference in steel temperature between the near and far ceiling in the matched run, and about 260 K in Run 1.
- **This agreement is a consistency check, not a validation.** The FDS heat release rate was prescribed, and Run 2 was sized to resemble the parametric fire in energy and duration, so close agreement is partly built in. No experimental data were used.

## Method

1. **ISO 834:** standard temperature–time curve, used as the reference for equivalent-time calculations.
2. **EN 1991-1-2 Annex A:** parametric fire calculated from fire load (511 MJ/m², office, 80 % fractile), ventilation and enclosure thermal inertia (b ≈ 1800 J/m²s½K).
3. **FDS:** two prescribed propane fires — Run 1 at 0.96 MW (constant after 150 s) and Run 2 at 5 MW (grows to 300 s, constant to 1500 s, decays to zero at 2100 s, about 8000 MJ in total). Thermocouples are placed above the fire and away from it. Uniform mesh, 0.2 m cells.
4. **Steel temperature:** EN 1993-1-2 lumped-capacitance method, 1 s time step, with gas temperature histories from each of the three descriptions as input.
5. **Equivalent time:** the duration of ISO 834 exposure that gives the same peak steel temperature in the same model.

## Reproducing the analysis

**Steel and equivalent-time analysis (no FDS needed).** The FDS output CSVs are included, so the analysis can be reproduced without re-running FDS. You need Python 3 with NumPy and Matplotlib. Keep the scripts and CSV files in the same directory, because the scripts use relative paths. Then run, in order:

```
python parametric_curve.py
python analyse_all.py
python make_figs.py
```

`make_figs.py` expects the FDS CSV outputs to be present and copies the figures generated by the first two scripts into `figures/`.

**FDS runs.** The input files were run with FDS 6.11.1 (Smokeview 6.11.2). They can be re-run with a compatible FDS installation, for example `fds compartment3.fds`. The runs take several hours, and the FDS output files are not included in the repository.

## Limitations

- Idealised geometry, a single compartment and prescribed fire sources. No structural analysis of a member is included, so strength reduction factors apply to the material only.
- Run 2 was sized to resemble the parametric fire, so agreement between the two is not independent of that choice. It is not the Annex E medium-growth fire: it grows faster and peaks higher, and some of it probably burns outside the door.
- Mesh sensitivity was checked only for Run 1, over the first 60 s (about 0.15 MW) at 0.1 m cells. No check was made for Run 2, and the full-duration refined run was not practical.
- Thermocouple readings are not true gas or steel-surface temperatures, and above the 5 MW fire they do not capture flame heat flux to a member.
- The section factor, convective heat-transfer coefficient and surface emissivity are assumed values. The lumped-capacitance model ignores temperature gradients within the section.
- Annex A assumes a mainly cellulosic fire load, while the FDS fires burn propane.
- No experimental validation of any model.
